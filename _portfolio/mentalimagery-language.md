## Abstract
In my very first essay on mental imagery, I line up personal accounts of non-imagers (or aphantasics) on how they understand their lack of imagery, along with objective measurements of mental imagery (e.g., the VVIQ or the Mental Rotation Task). I take a rather critical tone on both, following Schwitzgebel's arguments (2002), in that the concept of mental imagery is a difficult one. I then continue dissecting the embodied simulation theory (and its empirical support), according to which language understanding comes through mentally simulating the contents of language. Finally, the topic of language comprehension is connected to aphantasia; whether they might simulate language (or not), whether they might be verbalisers (as opposed to visualisers), or even whether they might use an abstract language of thought (the Mentalese). The essay concludes that mental imagery in language comprehension has at least two problems; one that is of interpretation and one that is of methodology.

## Why this work matters?
The problem of mental imagery in language comprehension is rooted in the general problem of mental imagery; how it is interpreted and explained. The critical tone I take in this essay grounds future research ideas, such as the comparison between mental simulations and mental images.

## Open questions remaining
* What experimental paradigms would best investigate aphantasia's language comprehension?
* How could qualitative and quantitative techniques complement each other in mental imagery and language comprehension studies?
* Would brain imagining studies show different neural processes in language comprehension between aphantasics and non-aphantasics?

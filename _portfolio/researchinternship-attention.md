## Abstract
Previous research shows that transient covert attention – when an external stimulus catches our attention without overt eye movements – can alter visual perception. Examples are increased contrast perception, increased spatial frequency, or change in object size. However, whether transient covert attention’s effects on contrast perception can be rendered unconscious or not remains an open question. Therefore, this research set up to first directly replicate an experimental paradigm by Carrasco et al. (2004), and second to pilot an unconscious version of it. In the original paradigm, a cue stimulus is briefly presented either in the middle or on the periphery of the fixation cross, which is then followed by the target stimuli: two Gabor patches in varying contrasts, presented on both sides of the fixation cross. Participants indicated the direction of the higher contrast target patch. In the unconscious version, the target stimuli were occluded by a “sandwich” mask design. As predicted, cuing participants as to where to focus their attention affected on how they perceived the contrasts of the targets. This was, however, not the case in the unconscious version, where the results were inconsistent. Alternative explanations are discussed, such as different versions of masking for the unconscious paradigm or whether attention itself could be rendered as an unconscious process or not.

## Why this work matters?
This study was reported in the form of a project report, not exclusively in the form of a research paper. Nevertheless, I found it relevant to include it in my portfolio for two reasons. On the one hand, the attentional paradigm was replicated successfully, which in itself strengthens the idea that our covert attention can alter the way we perceive the external world. On the other hand, it has real-life implications. Imagine (pun intended) approaching a pedestrian crossing as you sit behind the wheel. Imagine as a ball appears on the side, rolling towards the front of your car. Imagine as a child runs towards that ball. You will react faster to hit the pedal and stop the car as your attention was "primed" by the ball, as opposed to not having a ball in the first place. Your visual perception (fortunately) was altered by your attention.

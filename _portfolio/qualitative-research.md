## Abstract
In my first study with aphantasia, a qualitative approach is used (e.g., interpretivism or contructivism). A key concept is the transparency hypothesis, according to which our attention is never on our lived experience but on the object of our experience. This applied to mental imagery then becomes the main tenet of the study: experiencing a mental image we only focus on its _content_ but not on its _form_, therefore we interpret our mental images (or the lack of it). To investigate how people with aphantasia describe and interpret their experiences, a semi-structured interview was conducted online with four aphantasic participants. Questions during the interview included general experiences, specific instances from the past, and two imagery-related tasks. A thematic analysis revealed that these participants do not see "pictures" in front of them, not even with eyes closed; that a lack of imagery is multimodal (e.g., visual, auditory, olfactory); that they tend to "think of" or "know" things others may visualise; that they were surprised when they found out that others have these "pictures", even though not having them always felt normal to them; and some even pointed out the advantages of not having mental imagery. The study concludes that even though it might be difficult to describe our lived experiences, the interpretation one employs must always be taken seriously.

## Why this work matters?
While the study of mental imagery is not a new field, investigating the experiences of aphantasia is only about 10 years old. Within this emerging topic, the quantitative approach is the most common; relatively few qualitative accounts are available. Since mental imagery can be a difficult concept, it is important to study it from multiple angles.
